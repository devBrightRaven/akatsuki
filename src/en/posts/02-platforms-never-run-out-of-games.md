# The Platform Never Told Me to Buy Every Game. It Just Keeps the Next One in View.

I opened Steam because I wanted to find one game I could play tonight.

Ten minutes later, I still had not launched anything. My wishlist had grown by three, and my account had gained one more game discounted within an inch of its life, the kind that made not buying it feel like the waste.

It is not that I do not want to play. I keep looking for the next game precisely because I do. Finding one is easy. Starting one still means deciding which game gets tonight's time and energy.

## How the Next Game Stays in View

A digital game store is always open. Homepage recommendations, wishlist notifications, seasonal sales, and limited-time discounts take turns putting new choices in front of me. Outside the store, streams, reviews, and game clips on social media can suddenly make me want something I had not noticed before.

After discovering a game, I might read reviews, add it to my wishlist, and wait until the price feels right. The waiting has not disappeared. During that wait, the platform simply brings the game back into view again and again.

Steam's homepage draws on the games in a player's account, the games they have played, their purchase history, and what their friends are playing. A wishlist can send an email or notification when a game launches or reaches the discount threshold for a notice.

If a game is already included in a subscription or temporarily available for free, there is one less moment of hesitation over the price of that individual game. However it happens, the next game remains easier to keep under consideration.

## Every Feature Has a Reason

These features serve a real purpose. With so many games available, many would disappear from view without categories and notifications. Game teams need visibility, and players can discover work they would otherwise miss. A wishlist is also more convenient than trying to remember everything myself.

The issue is not that any one of these features is wrong. It is the gap they create when they accumulate.

Platforms are good at answering, "What might you like next?" They do not know whether I have enough energy to begin tonight. Purchases, claims, and wishlist additions leave clear numbers behind. When I meant to play, how long it took me to forget why I was interested, and whether I had the energy to begin are much harder to find in transaction data.

One game can reach countless players at once. Every instance of play still requires one person to invest their own time and feeling before the experience can actually happen.

## The Choice That Gets Deferred

The platform did not order me to buy every game. A recommendation, notification, or discount may genuinely lead me to something I love.

But when every step makes the next game easier to bring into view, more games are left for the future to decide. Adding one to my account takes only a moment. Whether to download it, when to begin, and whether I still remember why I wanted it will have to be decided again by a future version of me with enough time and energy.

That leaves me with another question. When a system keeps preparing the next option for me, I save the effort of searching. Do I also become less likely to stop and ask whether this was what I meant to do in the first place?

The next game is always in view. What gets deferred is how I intend to treat it.
